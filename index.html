Group 82: The Precise Observers
5-minute read

Can Money Buy
Olympic Success?

An investigative analysis into the correlation between national GDP and Olympic success using data from 1960 to 2016.

Published

May 12, 2026

Authors

Nick Tahmasebi, Filip Sahakian, Destin Zhong

T he Olympics are one of the most detailed records of global competition. Every four years, medals are awarded, counted, and added to national totals. Over time, a pattern seems to emerge: richer countries often dominate the podium — but how true is that relationship? This project examines the link between Olympic success, GDP, and population from 1960 to 2016. It asks a simple question:does economic power actually translate into medals, or is the reality more uneven?

The analysis covers all Olympic Games between 1960 and 2016, spanning the Cold War era through to modern global competition. While a small group of countries consistently dominates the medal tables, there are also repeated outliers that challenge simple economic explanations.

The dataset combines Olympic medal results with countrylevel GDP and population figures over the same period. Medal counts represent official Olympic outcomes, while GDP and population act as baselines to test whether sporting success scales with wealth or demographic size.

The Olympic Hierarchy

At first glance, Olympic success appears heavily concentrated. Between 1960 and 2016, a relatively small group of countries consistently dominated the medal table, many of them also among the world’s largest economies.

The similarity between these patterns raises a broader question: how much of Olympic success can actually be explained by economic power alone?

Measuring Olympic Efficiency

Raw medal counts only tell part of the story. Countries do not compete on equal economic footing some enter the Olympics with vastly larger economies and populations than others. To account for this imbalance, this project introduces a simple comparative metric: Olympic efficiency.

Definition

Efficiency Score = Actual Medals Won / Expected Medals Based on GDP

Expected medals are calculated from a country's share of global GDP in a given Olympic year. In other words:

“If Olympic medals were distributed purely according to economic power, how many medals would each country be expected to win?”

Example: United States (2016)

• Roughly 25% of global GDP

• Approximately 900 Olympic medals available

• Expected medals: 225

• Actual medals won: 121

Efficiency Score = 0.54

The United States won fewer medals than its economic share alone would predict.

Example: Kenya (2016)

• Roughly 0.04% of global GDP

• Approximately 900 Olympic medals available

• Expected medals: 0.36

• Actual medals won: 13

Efficiency Score = 36

Kenya dramatically exceeded what its economic size alone would predict.

Source: Olympic athlete data (Kaggle) & World Bank GDP data. Minimum threshold: 3 Olympic Games and 10 total medals.

What the Metric Reveals

Olympic success looks very different once wealth is accounted for.

Adjusting medal counts relative to GDP changes the Olympic hierarchy dramatically. Countries that dominate the raw medal table no longer appear automatically successful when measured against their economic size.

Nations such as Georgia, Armenia, Mongolia consistently outperform expectations, winning far more medals than their share of global GDP would predict. Meanwhile, several large economies perform much closer to or below their expected baseline.

The efficiency score therefore measures something different from total dominance. Rather than asking which country wins the most medals, it asks which countries convert economic resources into Olympic success most effectively.

Interpretation

Score = 1.0 → A country wins exactly the number of medals predicted by its GDP share.

Score > 1.0 → A country outperforms its economic weight.

Score < 1.0 → A country underperforms relative to its economic size.

Does Wealth or Population Matter More?

GDP and population both appear closely tied to Olympic success, but their relationships with efficiency are surprisingly weak. Once medal counts are adjusted relative to economic size, the expected patterns begin to break down.

Analysis

At first glance, both wealth and population appear strongly connected to Olympic success. Large economies dominate total medal counts, and countries with massive populations often produce more athletes and broader sporting infrastructure.

However, once medal totals are normalized using the efficiency metric, these relationships weaken substantially. The trend lines remain relatively flat, suggesting that neither GDP nor population alone is sufficient to explain which countries consistently outperform expectations.

The dashed reference line on the Population vs Olympic Efficieny 4.1 medals per million people represents the dataset-wide average efficiency. It is calculated by dividing the total medals won by all countries in the dataset (across every Olympic Games from 1960–2016) by the combined average population of those countries measured in millions.

In other words, the line represents the expected medal output if Olympic success were distributed purely in proportion to population size. Countries positioned directly on the line perform exactly in line with global demographic expectations, while countries above the line outperform their population weight and countries below it underperform relative to their size.

Some of the strongest Olympic overperformers are relatively small or middle income nations, such as Hungary and Jamaica, while several extremely wealthy countries cluster near or below the expected baseline. The result suggests that factors beyond pure economic scale such as sporting culture, specialization, and historical investment all play a major role in Olympic outcomes.

One caveat is that this is not a true world population average. The dataset only includes countries that participated in at least three Olympic Games and won at least one medal, excluding many smaller or non-participating nations. As a result, the average efficiency value is likely slightly higher than a true global baseline.

Olympic Success Has Different Shapes

Medal counts alone hide an important distinction: countries do not succeed in the same way. Some dominate across dozens of sports, while others concentrate their success within a small number of highly specialized events.

Comparative Fingerprints

Nations develop distinct Olympic identities

This comparison tool visualizes how countries distribute their Olympic success across different sports. Rather than measuring total medals alone, it highlights where nations specialize and how concentrated their strengths become over time.

Wealthier countries often display broader Olympic “fingerprints,” competing successfully across a wide range of disciplines including swimming, athletics, rowing, cycling, and gymnastics. Smaller or lower-income nations, by contrast, frequently concentrate their success within a handful of sports.

Kenya and Ethiopia, for example, are heavily centered around distance running, while countries such as the United States, China, and Russia exhibit far wider distributions across the Olympic program.

The result reveals that Olympic dominance is not only about how many medals a country wins, but also about the diversity and structure of the sports in which those medals are earned.

Source: Olympic athlete data (Kaggle) & World Bank GDP classifications. Medal distributions aggregated across Olympic Games from 1960–2016.

Conclusion

So, can money buy Olympic success?

Partly. Wealth creates the conditions for Olympic success, funding infrastructure, coaching, and athlete development. The richest nations do dominate the raw medal table, and the correlation between GDP and total medals is real.

But the efficiency metric tells a different story. Once medals are adjusted for economic size, the hierarchy collapses. Kenya, Georgia, Jamaica, and Mongolia consistently outperform countries with economies dozens of times larger. Meanwhile, several of the world's wealthiest nations win far fewer medals than their GDP alone would predict.

The data suggests that Olympic success is not bought it is built. Specialization, sporting culture, and focused investment in a small number of disciplines explain more of the variance than raw economic power. Money helps, but it does not decide.
