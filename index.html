Can Money Buy
Olympic Success?

An investigative analysis into the correlation between national GDP and Olympic success using data from 1960 to 2016.

Published

May 12, 2026

Authors

Nick Tahmasebi, Filip Sahakian, Destin Zhong

T he Olympics are one of the most detailed records of global competition. Every four years, medals are awarded, counted, and added to national totals. Over time, a pattern seems to emerge: richer countries often dominate the podium — but how true is that relationship? This project examines the link between Olympic success, GDP, and population from 1960 to 2016. It asks a simple question: does economic power actually translate into medals, or is the reality more uneven?

The analysis covers all Olympic Games between 1960 and 2016, spanning the Cold War era through to modern global competition. While a small group of countries consistently dominates the medal tables, there are also repeated outliers that challenge simple economic explanations.

The dataset combines Olympic medal results with countrylevel GDP and population figures over the same period. Medal counts represent official Olympic outcomes, while GDP and population act as baselines to test whether sporting success scales with wealth or demographic size.

The Olympic Hierarchy

At first glance, Olympic success appears heavily concentrated. Between 1960 and 2016, a relatively small group of countries consistently dominated the medal table — many of them also among the world’s largest economies.

Global wealth is distributed extremely unevenly. Across the 1960–2016 period, economic output remained concentrated in a relatively small number of countries — particularly the United States, Western Europe, and later East Asia. Much of the world competed in the Olympics with only a fraction of the economic resources available to the largest economies.

The similarity between these patterns raises a broader question: how much of Olympic success can actually be explained by economic power alone?

Measuring Olympic Efficiency

Raw medal counts only tell part of the story. Countries do not compete on equal economic footing — some enter the Olympics with vastly larger economies and populations than others. To account for this imbalance, this project introduces a simple comparative metric: Olympic efficiency.

Definition

Efficiency Score = Actual Medals Won / Expected Medals Based on GDP

Expected medals are calculated from a country's share of global GDP in a given Olympic year. In other words:

“If Olympic medals were distributed purely according to economic power, how many medals would each country be expected to win?”

Example: United States (2016)

• Roughly 25% of global GDP

• Approximately 900 Olympic medals available

• Expected medals: 225

• Actual medals won: 121

Efficiency Score = 0.54

The United States won fewer medals than its economic share alone would predict.

Example: Kenya (2016)

• Roughly 0.04% of global GDP

• Approximately 900 Olympic medals available

• Expected medals: 0.36

• Actual medals won: 13

Efficiency Score = 36

Kenya dramatically exceeded what its economic size alone would predict.

Source: Olympic athlete data (Kaggle) & World Bank GDP data. Minimum threshold: 3 Olympic Games and 10 total medals.

What the Metric Reveals

Olympic success looks very different once wealth is accounted for.

Adjusting medal counts relative to GDP changes the Olympic hierarchy dramatically. Countries that dominate the raw medal table no longer appear automatically successful when measured against their economic size.

Nations such as Georgia, Armenia, Mongolia consistently outperform expectations, winning far more medals than their share of global GDP would predict. Meanwhile, several large economies perform much closer to or below their expected baseline.

The efficiency score therefore measures something different from total dominance. Rather than asking which country wins the most medals, it asks which countries convert economic resources into Olympic success most effectively.

Interpretation

Score = 1.0 → A country wins exactly the number of medals predicted by its GDP share.

Score > 1.0 → A country outperforms its economic weight.

Score < 1.0 → A country underperforms relative to its economic size.

Analysis

Across twenty years, the four crimes do not move as one. Burglary and robbery track broader economic cycles, rising through the mid-2010s before pulling back later in the decade. Prostitution falls sharply and never fully recovers — a drop driven as much by shifting enforcement priorities as by any change in actual prevalence.

Arson stays low and flat throughout, dwarfed in volume by the other three. Isolating individual crimes using the legend makes the scale differences stark: at peak, burglary outnumbers arson by an order of magnitude.

Prostitution falls sharply and never fully recovers — a drop driven as much by policy as by any change in actual prevalence. The steepest decline coincides with the 2018 passage of FOSTA-SESTA, a federal law targeting online sex work advertising that pushed the trade off-street and largely out of SFPD's incident reporting system. The data shows fewer reports, not necessarily less activity.
